Insights / Methods and continuity

The handover should preserve more than the final number

Model files show what was run. They do not always show why. Reproducible analysis is a commercial asset when a project is sold, financed, or reviewed.

The idea in brief

The problem

A project changes hands, a new adviser arrives, or an investment committee asks why the P50 changed. The files show what was run, but not why.

Why it happens

The final number travels easily. The version history, uncertainty basis, settings, assumptions, and source metadata often stay behind in people’s heads and folders.

What to do

Treat the analytical chain as part of the deliverable: structure it so someone who was not in the room can understand, rerun, and challenge it.

Starting OBRAVEN has given me something I have not really had for a long time: the opportunity to choose my analytical tools without inheriting a toolbox built by somebody else.

There is no corporate licence or established workflow that ten other people depend on. No internal database or template library that has to remain backwards-compatible with what we built five years ago.

That freedom feels good. It is also slightly uncomfortable.

I know many of the established tools well. Have used some of them for years. But if I am going to spend my own money and build workflows around something now, I want to understand why I am choosing it. So I started looking again.

What do I need for measurement data analysis, long-term correction, wind flow modelling, layout optimisation, turbine interaction and blockage modelling, energy yield, losses, and uncertainty estimation? Which established tools are still relevant? Which newer platforms are solving something genuinely different, or do the same things better and faster? What should I commit to now, what should I be ready to activate when a project needs it, and what should I keep an eye on? The practical questions.

It did not stay a software buying exercise for long, though. Once I started speaking to developers and vendors, looking at newer cloud platforms, revisiting the established analytical packages, and following some of the standards and open-data work underneath them, I found myself looking at the whole chain again. Quite a rabbit hole, which kept branching in all directions.

The wider view turned out to be more interesting than the individual tool decision.

The change is not simply desktop to cloud

The easy story would be that established desktop software is gradually being replaced by cloud platforms, which will in turn be replaced by AI.

I don’t think that is what is happening.

Some established analytical tools are still developing quite actively. APIs, repeatable workflows, time-series processing, and better interfaces increasingly allow mature calculation engines to participate in automated workflows rather than requiring every analysis to be driven manually through a desktop interface.

One of the more useful conclusions from revisiting tools I have known for years is that maturity is not the same thing as stagnation. In several cases, the analytical depth is still there, while the workflow around it is becoming easier to automate, connect, and scale.

At the same time, newer cloud platforms are widening the problem they address.

A layout tool does not necessarily stop at layout optimisation. Some newer environments extend into resource screening, wakes, site suitability, electrical design, costs, finance, storage, logistics, and other parts of project development. Some are also trying to carry information further downstream into construction and operations.

What interests me here is less whether any one calculation is better and more what happens to the workflow around it. A project can be explored across more scenarios, more disciplines can work from related information, and fewer handovers need to start by rebuilding the previous team’s context.

Specialist models are moving across boundaries as well. I have seen examples of operational modelling being connected further upstream with wind, metocean, and revenue questions. Again, the interesting part is the connection between analytical domains that have often lived separately.

So I do not see a clean generational replacement yet, but the boundaries are softening.

Established engines are becoming easier to automate. Cloud platforms are spanning more of the development process – used by various teams – not only energy analysts. Specialist models are connecting to adjacent disciplines. And underneath all of this, there is a quieter effort to make the information passing between them more structured – which may matter more than which individual application wins.

Too often, the number goes further than the context behind it.

A layer I had underestimated: making the analysis transferable

Years ago, I completed a series of data science courses on Coursera. One of the ideas that stayed with me was reproducible research.

The basic idea was not especially exotic: somebody else should be able to start from the same source material, understand what you did, and reproduce the result. The code, data, assumptions, and method should belong to the analysis rather than existing partly in the analyst’s head.

It had quite a strong effect on how I subsequently thought about analytical work.

I started caring a lot about workspace structure, source files, intermediate outputs, naming, keeping assumptions visible, making a piece of work understandable to somebody who had not created it, and being able to pick it up again myself six months later without reconstructing my own thinking.

It matters inside a team. It matters more when work crosses teams.

And it matters enormously when a project changes hands.

That is one reason some of the less glamorous digitalisation work I have been reading about now looks quite important to me.

  • The IEA Wind Task 43 WRA Data Model gives measurement station metadata a machine-readable structure: location, instrumentation, mounting, logger configuration, and how those properties change through time. Its stated mission is to make energy yield assessment more efficient, transparent, and reproducible.
  • IEC 61400-15-1, published in 2025 for site suitability input conditions, now has something similar sitting beside the written standard: complementary human-readable Excel and machine-readable JSON forms carrying the same site condition information.
  • IEC 61400-15-2 is taking this further into wind resource and energy yield assessment. It is still in development, but its published scope is particularly relevant here: a framework covering assessment methods, uncertainty, reporting, traceability, and a digital exchange format. One of its stated aims is to make assessments more reproducible and easier to compare between different parties.
  • As part of that direction, the EYA Digital Exchange Format is trying to make the reporting layer machine-readable too. It is still explicitly a draft, and its own repository says parts remain incomplete. But the intended use cases are revealing: automated sharing, traceability, loading results into internal systems, comparing assessments from different parties, and passing structured information onwards to lenders and investors.
  • There is parallel work on turbine power curves. The public Power Curve Schema is a working area for a common machine-readable format associated with IEC 61400-16. The commercially sensitive values do not need to become public; the point is to agree the structure in which authorised parties exchange them.
  • Further into computational modelling, windIO provides a common definition framework for wind turbine and wind plant model inputs and outputs under IEA Wind Task 55. Version 2.0 was released in July 2025, with 2.1.1 following in February 2026.
  • ENTR Foundation is another open-source initiative I found interesting. It is a US non-profit working on open data standards, tools, and analytics for renewable energy. Its data stack is practical: common ways of structuring and naming tabular data, a shared warehouse model, and a framework for reusable analytical applications.

I have also recently joined the IEC/TC 88/WG15 work through the Irish standards route, with my role at this stage mainly to review and comment. Better late than never – the heavy drafting work has already been done by others over many years. My contribution will naturally be modest, but this is very close to the questions I am looking at here, so I am glad to have the opportunity to review the work and contribute where useful.

These are different initiatives under different governance. I would not call them all standards.

But together they show a direction of travel: more of the analytical chain is being given explicit structure instead of sitting in bespoke spreadsheets, PDFs, and undocumented conventions.

Why that matters outside the data team

The benefits are easy to underestimate if the discussion stays at the level of JSON schemas and software architecture. Consider what happens when somebody else has to pick up the work.

In technical due diligence, you often receive a report, appendices, spreadsheets, perhaps a model workspace, and a collection of supporting files.

Before you can challenge the result, you first have to establish what the result actually represents.

Which turbine revision was used? Which measurement period? Which long-term reference? What was filtered? Which wake model and settings? Which loss sits inside which number? Which assumptions changed between revisions? Where did the uncertainty come from? Is this apparent difference from another assessment methodological, numerical, or simply a different definition of the same thing?

That reconstruction can consume a lot of senior time before the interesting work starts.

The same problem exists within developer teams too. A project can move between analysts or offices. People leave. Consultants change. The layout evolves. A new turbine is added to the shortlist. A financing process brings another adviser into the conversation. Somebody asks why the current P50 is different from the number presented to the investment committee months earlier.

If the analytical history is well structured, that is a manageable question.

If it is distributed across files, emails, and people’s memories, it becomes archaeology.

That is why I think reproducibility matters commercially too. A reproducible analytical chain creates continuity. It gives the next analyst a better chance of understanding the latest answer and how the project got there. Internal review gets quicker. Cross-team collaboration needs less translation because fewer local conventions have to be learned first. And when a project is sold, financed, or independently reviewed, the receiving party starts from a better place, faster.

Common formats won’t make two assessments equally good, of course. A schema can require a field, check a unit, reject malformed data, preserve an identifier and a version. It won’t decide whether the measurement is representative, the model – suitable, an uncertainty – justified, or the conclusion – conservative enough for the decision being made. That distinction is important.

Better digital infrastructure should make it easier to apply judgement but does not remove the need for it.

I had seen part of this problem before

This made me think again about an idea I submitted to an internal DNV innovation programme in late 2017 or early 2018. It was a small idea.

We were already doing quick, high-level energy assessments manually. My proposal was to deliver our analysis in the browser, and let a client make a simple change – a revised layout, for example – and get a screening-level recalculation without waiting for an analyst to work through the same sequence again.

I thought the main challenge was putting our analytical logic into software. In retrospect, I was looking at the calculation and not enough at what other information had to go with it, and in what form.

Which version of the turbine data? Which model assumptions? What had happened to the wind data? Which losses were fixed and which were recalculated? Which parts were suitable for self-service and which required an analyst to intervene? How would you reproduce the previous run exactly? How would you know two scenarios were actually comparable?

The idea itself may or may not have been useful.

What I find interesting now is that many of the things I had not thought through are exactly the things being worked on across the industry: structured metadata, common model definitions, machine-readable reporting, validation, and interoperable workflows.

The calculation was only one piece.

Standardisation is expanding, but unevenly

I initially tried to draw one neat hierarchy of standards and formats. The landscape is quite diverse. Some areas have mature IEC standards. Others are draft exchange formats. Some are open-source data models. Some are research software. Some are pre-normative collaborations that help build evidence or common practice. There is overlap too, and several initiatives approach neighbouring parts of the same problem from different directions. Even keeping track of what exists, what is mature, and how it all fits together is a challenge in itself.

Wind measurement is a good example. Formal IEC measurement standards, including IEC 61400-50-2 for ground-mounted remote sensing, sit alongside industry implementation and verification work. IEA Wind Task 52 has worked on large-scale deployment of wind lidar, while MEASNET released its Ground-Based Lidar Verification Procedure in May 2026 to support common interpretation and consistency.

Operational analysis has a different mix. IEC 61400-25-1 gives an overview of the principles and models used across the IEC 61400-25 communications series. IEC 61400-26-1 provides a standardised information model and metrics for availability, with IEC TS 61400-26-4 extending this into reliability. OpenOA, from the National Laboratory of the Rockies (formerly NREL), is different again. It is explicitly research software – a Python framework providing common data structures and operational analysis methods.

Model assurance is emerging as another part of the picture. IEC PAS 61400-60 is being developed as a framework for validation of computational models. In parallel, IEA Wind Task 57, JAM is working on joint assessment, benchmarking, validation, and confidence in the models used for design, deployment, and operational decisions.

And Task 43’s current digitalisation work goes beyond file formats. It includes FAIR data, ontologies, knowledge graphs, SCADA data use, and practical demonstrations intended to reduce data wrangling effort and improve interoperability. The RDA Wind Energy Community Standards Working Group reached a useful conclusion in its 2025 FAIR-data review too: there is still no widely recognised community ontology at sector level.

So while end-to-end standardisation appears further away on the horizon, I see more people working on the interfaces – and this matters because interfaces hide a lot of analytical friction.

Interoperability may be more valuable than finding one perfect platform

This has changed how I think about my own tool choice.

At first I was looking for winners. Which platform covers the most? Which one is technically deepest? Which one is fastest? Which one should become the centre of an OBRAVEN workflow?

The cost question is much harder for a small advisory too. I cannot simply buy several tools, keep them all available, and decide later which one fits. I need enough breadth to cover most of the work I may be asked to do, while keeping real depth in the areas where I want to specialise.

Independence, data control, and confidentiality matter as well.

I also do not want the tool I happen to own to start dictating the method I recommend.

That has made me less interested in finding one winner.

Different project decisions need different levels of evidence and different analytical depth. A fast early-stage comparison does not have to use the same environment as a finance-grade energy yield assessment. Operational analysis has different data and method requirements again. Trying to force all of that into one system may not be the best answer.

The alternative is a set of tools that are good at their respective jobs and can exchange information without destroying context. I now give much more weight to APIs, stable data structures, import/export quality, and versioning than I would have ten years ago.

It also reduces another risk: technological lock-in. If a project history can survive outside one software environment, it is easier to change tools later. If the meaning exists only inside the application, the application owns more of your analytical process than you may wish or realise. For me, that is becoming one of the bigger questions behind tool choice.

AI makes structure matter more

AI is part of this too, and the immediate opportunity is practical.

It is becoming much easier to generate small pieces of code, automate repetitive transformations, connect systems, and explore a much larger design space than an analyst would reasonably click through manually.

I am increasingly comfortable using AI to help build small, inspectable tools around deterministic calculations. If I need a reproducible calculation, I would rather have code I can test and rerun than ask a language model to produce the numerical answer conversationally.

The same applies to design-space exploration. Computing and automation can let us test many more combinations of turbine, layout, constraint, and assumption than we could when every scenario needed manual setup.

All this changes the analyst’s job but does not remove it.

Someone still has to decide what the objective function should be. Which constraints are real. Whether the models are valid in that part of the design space. Whether two apparently better scenarios are actually comparable. Whether the gain is material enough to matter commercially. Whether the uncertainty around the result overwhelms the apparent optimisation.

Good structure matters even more once AI is involved. A language model given a well-defined data model, explicit terminology, controlled assumptions, and a traceable project history has something solid to work with. Give it a collection of loosely related PDFs, spreadsheets, and undocumented numbers and it can still produce an impressively fluent answer – but relying on that fluency would make me nervous.

So what am I taking from all this?

I have not reached the conclusion that I should replace every tool I know with a new cloud platform. Almost the opposite.

Some established analytical tools still have substantial methodological depth and continue to develop. Where that depth and external familiarity matter, they still earn their place.

I do think newer workflow platforms are worth taking seriously. What attracts me is the speed of iteration, wider scenario exploration, easier collaboration, and the ability to connect decisions that used to sit in separate tools.

I am also paying much more attention to interoperability. Machine-readable measurement station metadata or structured EYA output can reduce retyping, preserve history, make comparisons cleaner, and let a project move between people and systems with less information loss. The cumulative value can be substantial.

And I want reproducibility to remain a design principle rather than a nice-to-have. If I create a workflow today, I want the answer, the assumptions, the source material, and the route between them to remain together as far as reasonably possible. Because projects live longer than analysts, teams, and sometimes companies.

That also changes where I think senior energy analysts add value. Less time operating software by habit and moving information between applications. More time choosing the right evidence for the decision, understanding where a model is trustworthy, challenging assumptions, designing useful sensitivities, interpreting uncertainty, and recognising when a fast answer requires someone to stop and look at it properly.

That is broadly where I want to arrive. I am still refining the map. Some of the initiatives above are mature; some are drafts; some may go in directions I have not anticipated. I have deliberately tried not to turn this into a league table of software or a catalogue of every digitalisation project in wind.

It is simply what I can see from the position I am in now: an experienced user of the existing analytical environment who, for once, does not have to inherit it.

If you are working on one of these initiatives, use something I have missed, or think I have put a piece in the wrong place, I would genuinely welcome the correction.

Part of the reason for putting this in public is to improve my own map before I commit more time and money to it.

Where this applies – and where it does not

Most valuable where analysis changes hands: acquisitions, financings, adviser changes, internal team moves, and long development programmes. For a one-off screening study the full chain is more than is needed; a short note with the basis and the main assumptions is enough. The standards listed are dated October 2026 and some are still drafts.

Questions for your next project meeting

  1. If our analyst left tomorrow, could someone rerun the current case from the files alone?
  2. Can we explain why today’s P50 differs from the one in the last investment paper?
  3. When we receive a seller’s EYA, how long does it take just to establish what it represents?
  4. Is our measurement metadata recorded in a form that survives a change of supplier or software?
