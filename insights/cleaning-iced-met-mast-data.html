Cleaning iced met mast data: the decisions that change the energy yield

Icing turns met mast cleaning into a judgement call. Most of those calls barely touch the energy yield; a few change it, and you can test which.

The idea in brief

The problem

“Does it really matter how aggressively we clean the data?”

Why it happens

Icing is messy: sensors ice and recover unevenly, analysts flag it differently, and what is removed is never a random sample of the year.

What to do

Test the doubtful periods – keep, remove, reconstruct – against the energy yield and its uncertainty, and write down the calls that change it.

A while back, when there was much less automation than we can have today, we gave the same heavily iced met mast dataset to four analysts.

We got four quite different cleaned series back.

Nobody had been careless. Each of them made reasonable calls on the stretches where the data was ambiguous, and those calls added up to four different versions of the same mast.

Those differences start at the cleaning stage, before any modelling. So it's a good place to ask the question one of the commenters on a recent thread raised: does it really matter how aggressively we clean?

Icing is messy, and the mess is physical

There are two clocks running in an icing event. Meteorological icing is the period when weather conditions favour ice building up on exposed surfaces. Often that's below freezing, with supercooled water in cloud, fog or freezing rain. Wet snow can also stick to instruments at temperatures just above zero. Instrumental icing is the period when ice on the sensor interferes with its measurements.

The second clock can run much longer. Ice may remain on a sensor after the icing weather has gone, until it melts, sublimates, erodes or sheds. Early estimates for the Swiss Jura put instrumental icing at about four times the duration of meteorological icing.

Instrumental icing is the period you're cleaning, and it doesn't switch on and off neatly. On a cold day a sensor can go iced, not iced, iced again - cloud comes down on the mast, the sun comes out, the temperature creeps above zero.

The sensors don't fail the same way either:

  • A cup anemometer often reads low before it stops. Its standard deviation drops as the ice adds mass.
  • A vane gets stuck and then frees up again. It often freezes before the cup at the same height, and its standard deviation goes to zero.
  • An unheated ultrasonic anemometer can report falsely high wind speeds when its transducers ice up.
  • Heated sensors help a lot, but none stays ice-free in all conditions. A sensor with only its shaft heated can keep turning with ice on it and quietly read low, and a heated sensor can melt falling snow that then refreezes on it.
  • Cold on its own slows a cup as the bearing grease stiffens, so a slow cup on a cold day isn't always an iced one.

Different sensor types also ice at different rates. Their size, geometry, bearings and lubricant can all make a difference. In my experience the sequence often repeats from one event to the next. When sensors drop out one by one in roughly the same order, that's another sign pointing to icing, particularly when the other measurements support it.

If you catch yourself picking the version with the nicer number, stop.

Four ways to clean the same record

If you're flagging this by hand and you see dozens of transitions, picking through them one by one is a lot of work. Some people do it anyway. Others mark the whole lot as a suspect period and take it all out. If one cup is clearly iced, some assume the others of the same type are iced too and clean them out. And some leave it all in.

Even the recommended practice builds in judgement. The IEA's cold climate guidance suggests removing data for a number of hours before and after a suspected icing event, because icing builds slowly. How many hours is your call, and so is what counts as a suspected event.

Each gives a different series, and some calls are more defensible than others - it depends what the sensors actually show.

On the masts where I learned this, every winter brought icing. Flagging it well by hand was slow, it was subjective, and we argued about it. My boss at the time made us look closely at how each sensor behaved, and that's where most of what I know about it comes from. Later I worked across markets where icing never came up, and none of it mattered.

Does it matter? It depends how much icing you have

At a site with a few short events, say 0.1% of the record, I'd be fairly relaxed: flag it broadly and get on with the rest of the analysis. At 5%, the cleaning choices can affect the energy yield estimate, and being selective starts to matter.

Icing also varies much more from year to year than the annual mean wind speed, so one winter can give a misleading picture in either direction.

Two things carry the cleaning choice through to the yield:

  • What you take out is never a random sample of the year. Icing needs particular weather - cold, usually with cloud or fog on the mast - so removing it shifts the record towards other seasons and conditions, and the long-term correction only sees the concurrent data you give it.
  • What you leave in is biased too. Iced cups read low, and an iced ultrasonic can read high.

So the idea that the long-term correction and the wind flow model will wash it out is something to check, not assume.

Test the decisions that could matter

I don't think you can know which way it goes without testing it. Where reasonable analysts would disagree:

  1. Pick out the ambiguous stretches: the transitions, the margins either side of an event, and the sensors that look odd.
  1. Build the alternatives: keep the doubtful data, remove it, or reconstruct it from another sensor, such as a heated one or another height. Build any reconstruction on periods without icing, and check whether that relationship changes with the season.
  1. Run each version through the same long-term correction and energy yield.
  1. Compare the long-term mean wind speed, the net energy, and the uncertainty.

If nothing much changes, use a simple rule and don't lose sleep over it. If it does change, that's where the analyst's time should go: decide, write down why, and consider what the difference means for the uncertainty assessment.

One caution though. The test is there to show you where judgement is needed. If you catch yourself picking the version with the nicer number, stop.

Design the mast so there's less to argue about

The best time to deal with icing is before the mast goes up. The IEA's cold climate guidance asks, as a minimum, for icing to be measured at or above hub height: instrumental icing with a properly heated and an unheated anemometer, or an unheated vane, or meteorological icing with a camera or another validated method. It recommends assessing both, with more than one detection method running in parallel.

A few things I'd add from practice:

  • Keep good unheated cups as the primary sensors and use the heated ones for the icing periods. Heated sensors are generally less accurate in normal conditions.
  • Measure temperature and humidity, but don't treat "below zero and humid" as proof of icing. Used on its own as a detection rule, that combination significantly overestimates icing frequency. These measurements help screen the conditions, but you need other evidence to identify the affected records.
  • Heat the booms and keep sensors out of each other's way. In severe icing an iced boom spoils a heated sensor, and falling ice can damage whatever sits below it.
  • Measure icing for at least a year and relate it to the long term, because icing varies so much from year to year.

Icing climates are also hard on the sensors themselves. We'd refurbish or replace the cups every couple of winters, recalibrate them, and post-calibrate the ones coming down, and some had drifted. Every climate has its own version of this: in a dry desert the kit can last for years, and where lightning is frequent, it's the lightning protection that gets tested.

The more of these signals you read together - heated against unheated, the standard deviation, the order sensors drop out, temperature and humidity, and a camera image - the closer you get to the right answer.

Where automation helps, and where it stops

Automated cleaning helps with consistency. Rules that compare sensors and account for temperature can apply the same checks to every record, including the hundredth transition. Machine learning is another approach, but how well it works depends on the input data, the quality of its training and how it was validated. When I was first working with iced mast data, most of the cleaning was manual, with a few supporting rules.

Someone still has to check what it flags, especially on the ambiguous events. The few calls that really change the yield still need an analyst to make them and explain them.

What I'd want written down

  • which signals and thresholds flagged icing, and how they depend on temperature;
  • the margins taken either side of an event;
  • which sensors were removed, kept, or reconstructed, and why;
  • how any reconstruction was built and checked;
  • the sensitivity results: wind speed, energy, and uncertainty for each alternative;
  • which decisions changed the result, and how they were handled.

Ideally, another analyst could repeat your cleaning and see which decisions actually made a difference.

Where this applies – and where it does not

Applies wherever a measurement mast meets icing: in cloud or freezing fog at hub height, freezing rain, or wet snow, which is why it matters most on high or cold sites and grows as masts and hub heights get taller. It matters most where instruments are iced for more than a few percent of the year; on a site with a few short events, a simple, broad rule is usually enough. The same habit – test the doubtful periods against the energy yield and write down the calls that change it – carries to other judgement calls in data cleaning.

Questions for your next project meeting

  1. How much of our record was affected by icing, and how was it flagged?
  2. Which cleaning decisions did we test against the energy yield, and what changed?
  3. Do we have a heated and an unheated sensor at the same height, and how long has icing been measured?
  4. Where data was reconstructed, was the relationship built on periods without icing and checked by season?
  5. Could another analyst repeat our cleaning from what we have written down?
