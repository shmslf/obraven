The finance-stage EYA may be iteration 50

Most wind projects go through dozens of energy yield iterations before financing. The formal assessment should build on that history.

By Stan Herasymenko, Founder & DirectorFirst published on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab), 16 July 2026Website edition, October 2026

The idea in brief

The problem

A project asks for “the updated yield number” many times before financing. The request is short; the input list behind it is not.

Why it happens

The inputs come from many teams and mature at different speeds. Without a controlled basis, iterations drift, rework builds up, and the reasoning behind each number is lost.

What to do

Agree a short scenario summary before each material run, keep outputs proportionate, and let the finance-stage EYA inherit a clear record.

I have worked on wind projects that went through dozens of energy yield iterations before the finance-stage energy yield assessment (EYA).

Fifty is illustrative. One project may have twelve substantial iterations; another may have fifty smaller ones. One iteration might be a single wake rerun, while the next compares ten turbine, hub-height and layout scenarios.

The finance-stage EYA usually comes near the end of a much longer analytical process. The earlier work helps the project design its measurement campaign, choose which layout to progress, test credible turbine options, refine assessment of the main losses, and understand the key uncertainty drivers.

That energy analysis work should follow the project rather than disappear between an early yield estimate and a final bankable report several years later.

What the first 49 look like

A layout changes and the wake calculation is rerun. A new turbine is added to the shortlist and another batch of scenarios is compared. Hub height increases enough that the available measurement heights, coverage across the rotor-swept height range and vertical extrapolation assumptions need another look. A materially longer measurement record may justify revisiting the long-term adjustment.

Later iterations may replace provisional loss assumptions or add external wake scenarios as the design matures.

Most of these iterations do not need a full EYA report. The project team usually needs an updated net annual energy production (AEP) summary, inputs and assumptions used, what changed, key limitations and open risks, and recommended next steps.

A short technical note is often enough. A full finance-grade report after every change would add cost and delay, and much of it would go unread. The rigour should remain; the output can stay proportionate.

A material change becomes the next iteration.

The request is rarely as simple as it sounds

The initial request is often only a few lines long:

Here is the updated layout and the new turbine model. Can you update the yield number?

But the input list is actually longer.

Which boundary and layout are current? Which turbine revision, hub height, and operating mode apply? Which measurement and modelled data underpin the work? Does the measurement campaign still provide adequate spatial and vertical coverage for the current layout and expected rotor-swept height range, and is the wind-flow model still appropriate? Which electrical, grid, environmental, noise and curtailment inputs are fixed for this run, and which remain provisional?

For a large offshore project, that input list can run to several dozen lines. Onshore projects usually have fewer, although mixed terrain, planning constraints, noise limits, and evolving layouts can create a substantial interface problem of their own.

The energy analyst rarely owns all of those inputs. They come from teams focusing on development, spatial data, environmental, electrical, grid, turbine procurement, civil design and finance, as well as external specialists. Each is focused on its own workstream. Energy analysis is one of the places where their outputs have to meet.

The energy analyst ends up managing the scope

It took me time to learn that the energy analyst often has to manage this process rather than wait for somebody else to do it.

Earlier in my career, I was more likely to take the request at face value and begin modelling. In practice, nobody necessarily arrives with every interface resolved and every input lined up.

Without a deliberate scope check, the analysis can start too soon. A different turbine revision appears halfway through. A missing input takes weeks to obtain. Another team is working from an older layout. What began as a straightforward update turns into waiting, rework, and several versions of the answer.

No one may have formally assigned the energy analyst a project-management role, but it is usually the analyst who ends up holding it.

My practical response became what I call a scenario summary. It is a simple control sheet that records the basis for the current iteration:

Purpose of the iteration and decision being supportedSets the depth of work and the output needed

The value is in making the interfaces visible before the analysis starts. The summary gives the team a point to agree what is fixed for the iteration, what is provisional and what would trigger another run. In complex cases, it can be best to work through the list live in a workshop.

Once agreed, that basis should remain stable long enough to finish the work. A material change becomes the next iteration.

Project inputs mature at different speeds

The layout may be on version 12 while parts of the loss stack remain at screening level. The electrical design may already be well advanced while environmental, curtailment or operating-strategy inputs are still being developed.

For a time, that can be entirely reasonable. Broad assumptions are often the right choice early in development. Detailed assessment without the supporting project information creates false precision and unnecessary cost. There is little value in refining one loss assumption to the third decimal place while the turbine, layout and electrical design are still preliminary.

The valuable judgement here is knowing when a placeholder has done its job.

Some assumptions can stay broad for several more iterations. Others become material at the next decision point. A maturing electrical or grid design may justify project-specific loss assessment. A developing noise or curtailment strategy may need scenario-specific treatment, while a layout nearing consent deserves a different level of wake and constraint analysis from an early screening layout.

As the project matures, the analysis should deepen with it. That tests the central estimate, strengthens the evidence behind it and allows uncertainty to be estimated when the inputs support it.

The history behind the numbers

The scenario summary also becomes part of the project’s technical history.

That matters when the project is sold, responsibilities move between teams, or the analyst or consultant changes. A new person may inherit reports, spreadsheets, model workspaces, and folders containing years of energy analysis work.

Those files will show what was run, but not always carry enough context to show why one scenario was progressed, which assumptions were accepted temporarily, what had already been tested and rejected, or which input was expected to be revisited at the next stage.

Running the model again is often straightforward. Recovering the reasoning can take much longer. Here, a short iteration note, a controlled scenario summary and clear versioning preserve much of that reasoning without creating a large reporting burden. They also show whether the current layout, turbine, measurement dataset, long-term adjustment, wake model setup, loss factors and, where applicable, uncertainty assessment still describe the same project.

Analytical ownership may be internal, external, or shared. Either way, somebody needs to know what the current net AEP estimate represents, what changed since the previous iteration, and what needs attention next.

What iteration 50 should inherit

A finance-stage EYA usually brings deeper scrutiny and often independent review.

The independent engineer or finance-stage assessor may use different methods, challenge assumptions, request sensitivities, or have a different approach to uncertainty assessment.

Good development-stage work cannot guarantee that every conclusion will survive unchanged. It can, however, reduce avoidable surprises.

A gap in measurement duration, height coverage, or spatial representativeness discovered late often cannot be fixed within the project programme. Additional uncertainty may be assigned because required evidence was never collected and there is no time for it now. Generic loss factors can be challenged after they have already been firmly embedded in the financial model.

When the energy analysis has developed with the project, those issues are more likely to have been identified while the team still had choices.

The first 49 do not all need to be prepared for formal lender reliance. They do need sound methods, controlled inputs, clear assumptions, honest limitations, and outputs proportionate to the decision.

By financial close, the finance-stage EYA may be iteration 50. It should build on analysis that has followed the project through the first 49 iterations.

Where this applies – and where it does not

Most relevant to utility-scale onshore and offshore wind projects in development, where layout, turbine, and constraints change several times before financing. On a small single-turbine scheme, or a project bought late with a finished EYA, the scenario-summary idea still helps, but the number of iterations is smaller and the review question usually matters more.

Questions for your next project meeting

  1. What does our current net AEP number represent – which layout, turbine, data, and losses?
  2. Which of our loss assumptions are still placeholders, and by which stage does each need a project-specific value?
  3. Would a new analyst be able to see why the number changed between the last two iterations?
  4. Is there a measurement gap that will be impossible to close by the time financing starts?
