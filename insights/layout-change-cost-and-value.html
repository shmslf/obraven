Insights / Energy yield

A layout change can save cost and still lose value

Wind project value often sits between disciplines. A turbine move that helps civil design may quietly cost generation.

By Stan Herasymenko, Founder & DirectorFirst published on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab), 25 June 2026Website edition, October 2026

In this piece
  1. Where this applies
  2. Questions to ask

The idea in brief

The problem

A turbine is moved for sound reasons – a cleaner road, an easier platform, lower civil quantities.

Why it happens

The civil saving is visible quickly. The energy effect depends on exposure, wake interaction, spacing, and terrain-driven flow, and is easy to miss.

What to do

Run a light energy check on design revisions before the layout is frozen, and ask whether the project improved or only one workstream did.

A layout change can save cost and still lose value.

Recent proposal work has reminded me how often wind project value sits between disciplines, rather than neatly inside one of them.

A turbine move can start for very sensible reasons. A road alignment becomes cleaner. Access looks less painful. A platform sits better in the terrain. Civil quantities come down. A delivery route works with fewer compromises. From that part of the project, the change can be entirely rational.

The energy-yield impact can be less obvious.

Distance alone is a poor guide. A relocation matters when it changes the wind resource picture: exposure, wake interaction, turbine spacing, or terrain-driven flow.

On some sites, the energy effect will be negligible. On others, especially in more complex terrain or constrained layouts, a modest-looking relocation can change more than the drawing suggests.

Most design revisions do not need a full energy yield assessment while the project is still moving. A lighter check is often enough at that stage: what moved, why did it move, has the wake or exposure picture changed, and is the civil/access benefit likely to be larger than the energy-yield impact?

Sometimes the answer is yes. The move is fine, the saving is worth taking, and the project is stronger.

Sometimes the saving is real, but the project has quietly lost generation value, increased uncertainty, or made the later investment case harder to explain.

This is why I like energy analysis being brought into layout and access discussions early enough to be useful, and light enough not to slow the whole project down.

Before a layout is frozen, the question is simple: has the project improved, or has one workstream improved?

Where this applies – and where it does not

Most relevant in complex or forested terrain, tightly constrained layouts, and sites with strong directional wind regimes. On open, simple sites with generous spacing, small moves often have a negligible energy effect – the check confirms that quickly.

Questions for your next project meeting

  1. Which turbines moved in the last layout revision, and was the energy effect checked?
  2. Is anyone weighing civil savings against lifetime generation value in the same currency?
  3. Did any move change wake interaction with neighbouring turbines?
  4. When is the layout frozen, and what checks must happen before then?
